## HOW TO USE THIS

This journal is not a stats sheet. Those already exist — Inara, EDSM, EDMC keep the numbers for
her. This one keeps what the numbers don't.

**But it is also a physical object inside the story**, and that object has a history of its own:
a blank logbook, handed to a patient coming out of a four-year coma, by a doctor who called it
treatment. Not therapy in the abstract — a stack of blank pages and a pen, because the part of the
mind that remembers is sometimes reached faster through the hand than through the head.

She did not ask for it. She did not want it. That reluctance is not a flaw to write around — it
is the starting condition, and it has to show on the page.

### THE DOUBLE PURPOSE

*(added 2026-09-20, Tonton Marcel's framing — a lens for the craft decisions below, not a new rule
that competes with them)*

On the surface this is a political / plot sci-fi thriller: Powers, a conspiracy, a betrayal, a
woman piecing together who tried to have her killed. That's what puts a reader on the next page,
and it should never read as an alibi for something else going on underneath.

Underneath, the actual subject is a cyborg's cognition — a machine's approximation of a human mind,
examined from the inside, in its own hand, without ever naming itself as such. The thriller plot is
the vehicle. The question the logbook is actually answering, entry by entry, is what it costs to
fake — or become — a human mind convincingly enough that even its own narrator stops being able to
tell the difference.

Both readings have to work at once, and neither should need the other footnoted in:
- A reader chasing Powers, betrayal and the chase should get all of it, undiluted, nothing held
  back for the sake of the deeper layer.
- A reader who's noticed the cognition question — *is she choosing this, or running an algorithm?
  is the arc of the hand character growth, or a machine converging on spec?* — should find every
  clue was already sitting in plain sight, dressed as an ordinary character beat the first time
  through (see The Arc of the Hand, below; The Body Keeps the Log It Wasn't Given).

**Working rule:** never write a sentence that only makes sense as a cybernetics essay. If a beat
about her cognition can't also stand, unassisted, as a beat about a woman afraid of attachment, cut
it or fold it into one that does both.

### THE ARC OF THE HAND

This is the part that makes the logbook different from a mission log: **the writing itself gets
better as she does.** Not the events — the sentences. The instrument through which she tells the
story must visibly change as the story goes on, in three broad, overlapping movements. Don't
announce the shift. Let the prose demonstrate it.

**Movement one — refusal wearing a form.**
Early entries are short because she is *making* them short. Clipped sentences. Verbs more than
adjectives. She describes what she did, rarely what she felt, and when feeling leaks through it's
an accident, immediately covered by the next flat fact. The six-line format (below) is obeyed to
the letter and no further — sometimes only four lines get filled, and the blank space is the
tell. A page left half-empty is not a mistake to fix. It is what a reluctant hand looks like.

**Movement two — the crack.**
Somewhere past the first real fear, the first real cost, a sentence runs longer than she meant it
to. She notices things she didn't ask her hand to notice: a smell, a voice, the weight of a
silence. The sixth line — *what I didn't put in the official report* — starts arriving before the
other five, instead of after, as if that's the part she actually wanted to say and the rest is
scaffolding built around it. Entries get longer without her deciding to write more. She might even
comment on it, briefly, with visible irritation — a woman annoyed at her own pen for
betraying her.

**Movement three — relief.**
By the middle chapters, she is no longer filling in a form. She's writing because it has become
the only place she says things out loud. Full pages. Digressions she doesn't apologise for.
Description for its own sake — a colour, a sound, a piece of technical detail lingered over
because it's pleasurable to get right. The six-line skeleton survives only as an occasional
formal joke she plays with her own history, not as a cage. By Chapter VII or VIII, an entry might
run two pages and end on something close to tenderness — toward a place, a person, or, once, near
the very end, toward herself.

**Calibration rule:** the transition is not tied to a chapter number, it's tied to *cost*. Every
real loss, fear or unguarded moment pulls the prose one notch toward movement two or three; every
stretch of safe, procedural flying can pull it back half a notch. The arc is not a straight line.
It stutters. That's what makes it true.

### ENTRY FORMAT

Not a cage — a starting skeleton she's still gripping tightly in the early chapters, and one she
gradually stops needing.

```
### [Galactic date] — [System / Station]
Ship:
Mission:
Outcome: success / failure / half of both
What it cost me:
What I learned:
What I didn't put in the official report:
```

The sixth line is the only one that matters. It's where she writes what she was afraid to say —
and, later, what she's no longer afraid to say, which is its own kind of progress worth tracking.

**Three rules.**

1. **Failures get written down.** One botched infiltration is worth three successful deliveries,
   in literature as in memory.
2. **One entry per session, however short.** Three lines beat a page that never gets written.
   Especially early on — a short entry that was hard to produce is more honest than a long one
   forced into existence for the sake of the arc.
3. **Don't dress up the outcome, dress up the sensation.** How much she earned, the bank already
   knows.

## FILE — WHO SHE WAS
*(truth known to the reader, never to the character — she'll only recover it in pieces)*

**Origin.** Not from Cubeo. **LHS 3447** — the system where every pilot starts, the one you leave
and never come back to. No family she remembers. Possibly no family at all: the question stays
open, and has to stay open a long time.

**The trade.** She rarely turned down a job, including the illegal ones. But one code, never
written down, never discussed: save the widow and the orphan, punish the tyrants and those who
trade in human lives. An outlaw with a stricter morality than the judges.

**The allegiance.** That code is what led her to **Aisling Duval** — the abolitionist, the one who
promises her subjects a better life. No calculation, no ambition: a convergence. The Clippers, the
title, the drift toward Cubeo all follow from it. Missions that mattered, that paid well, and that
got more and more visible.

**The betrayal.** She became inconvenient to someone. She was sent against the Thargoids on a
mission sold as survivable, which it wasn't. Her modules' armour ratings were falsified — billed
as reinforced, they weren't. **The acid ate through them.** She wasn't prepared, and that was the
point.

**2026-09-18 — confirmed:** the hands that installed the falsified module were Roskam's. He was
hacked into doing it — compromised, not complicit — and has never chosen to tell her. Who reached
into him to make it happen is still unbuilt (see `characters.md`, "Characters to be built" → the
modules' supplier). Internal record only; not yet on the page.

**The rescue.** Cubeo's doctor suspected something wrong in Roskam before she understood the shape
of the mission itself — she'd known him for years, and it read to her like a compromised cyborg
going wrong. She was too late to stop the install, too late to stop the launch. She followed
anyway, holding the Viscountess's wake the whole way out, never quite closing the distance. She was
still chasing when the Viscountess reached the combat field. She arrived after the hull gave out,
and pulled the escape pod from the wreck herself. Four years of care followed.

**The open question.** Those falsified modules came from somewhere. Someone ordered them, someone
certified them, someone installed them. She remembers none of it: not the people, not the
missions, not the faces. She'll only relearn through **sensation** — what she feels around someone
before she understands why. The rest will come in fragments: contacts, rumours, truths, lies,
manipulations.

**2026-09-20 — added to the File (internal, not yet on the page):** she is herself a built pilot
(the doctor's work, ~20 years ago), and the logbook is partly the doctor's instrument for watching
her "product" develop. See Ch. III decision 9 (Through-Line, below).

## RECURRING CHARACTERS

### Cubeo's doctor
War physician. A seasoned pilot too — not a civilian; she knows what it means to fly toward
something that wants to kill you. Infinitely gentle, infinitely careful. She's the one who pulled
the pod out of the void and watched over it for four years.

She loves the Viscountess with a love that will never be returned, and she knows it. She asks for
nothing. The Viscountess, for her part, feels something — and runs, because attachment frightens
her more than Thargoids do. **There will be no physical relationship between them.** The whole
tension of the character lives in what stays unsaid.

Function: the conscience. She resurfaces whenever the Viscountess takes too many risks. And she
holds a piece of the truth about the betrayal — the piece she never dared hand over.

*Settled, 2026-09-18 (internal record — not yet on the page):* "a love that will never be returned"
is the present tense, not the whole history. Before the accident, during the Great Solitude (Dec
3306, `Galaxy Chronicles.md`), something real and briefly mutual almost happened between them and
didn't hold — the doctor remembers it; the Viscountess's amnesia erased it completely, which is
also why the Day 1 LHS 3447 question lands as loaded. See `characters.md`, Doctor of Cubeo §7.

### The old mechanic of Hangar 12
Her **real family**. Big brother, guardian, unshakeable support. His own name is **Sam** — he's
the one who calls her **Tio**, and that nickname is the name under which the galaxy knows her
(confirmed on the page: `Logbook.md`, Ch. I Day 2, "Ah. There you are, Tio."). He kept all four of
her hulls maintained during her absence. He always *knew* she'd come back. He flies with her as
long as the doctors forbid solo flight. The only person in front of whom her title carries no
weight at all.

*Settled, 2026-09-18 (internal record — not yet on the page):* he installed them. He was hacked
into doing it, didn't choose it, and carries a guilt he will never voice. Still open: who reached
into him to make it happen (see `characters.md`, the modules' supplier).

### The ducal contact
Go-between with Aisling. Polite, insistent, never quite candid. The closest thing to the source:
the important missions ran through people like him.

### The bartender of Medupe City
He serves without looking up. He hears everything. He may know who she was.

### The rival
A CMDR she's crossed paths with more than once. Maybe a Patreus loyalist — maybe not. Maybe an old
comrade.

### The ghost
Someone she knew before, whose face she can't recover. Never reveal this one too soon.

## THE BODY KEEPS THE LOG IT WASN'T GIVEN

A craft note, not a scene to write directly. She doesn't know she's a survivor of a targeted
betrayal — but the body doesn't need the story to react correctly. Hypervigilance, an exaggerated
startle, a racing pulse at a cue that looks nothing like the original threat, sleep that won't
hold, a guilt with no assignable cause: these are real, well-documented responses to exactly her
situation, and they're available on the page **only as physical fact, never as diagnosis.** She
will never think the word "trauma." She will notice her hands are cold on a warm concourse, that
a docking klaxon puts her back somewhere she can't name, that she slept badly and doesn't know
why. The reader, given enough of these without a label, will get there before she does — which is
the whole design of this journal (see "What we don't take," above).

## STYLE MANUAL

Two references, two distinct contributions. Don't mix them at random: Follett supplies the
**skeleton**, Jordan supplies the **texture**.

### What we take from Ken Follett — the mechanics

- **Transparent prose.** Short sentences, ordinary vocabulary, zero ornament. The beauty comes from
  what happens, never from how it's phrased. If a sentence draws attention to its own author, cut
  it.
- **One scene = one goal, one obstacle, one change.** If the situation is identical at the end of
  the entry, the entry serves no purpose. Even a cargo run has to cost or teach something.
- **End on a hook.** Last line = a turn, a partial revelation, or a threat. Never a summary.
- **Procedural detail as proof of reality.** Follett has his characters build cathedrals: you
  believe in a character because they know their trade. Here: power priority settings, the
  rotating docking slot of an Orbis station, the smell of a refinery run, the way you steady a
  probe. Technical competence *is* characterisation.
- **The reader knows more than the heroine.** In Follett, you watch the villain act before the
  victim understands. Here, that's the job of the **File**: we know about the modules; she doesn't.

### What we take from Robert Jordan — the texture

- **Physical tics as identity.** Every recurring character gets **one** gesture, repeated, never
  commented on. You recognise them before their name. Assignments:
  - The mechanic: *the rag he wipes his hands with and never finishes wiping them.*
  - The doctor: *her hand finding a wrist — a pulse-check reflex that became something else.*
  - The ducal contact: *(to be defined)*
- **The world through objects.** Fabrics, metals, lighting, food, heraldry. Jordan describes a room
  through what you touch in it. Here: dust, acid, the worn leather of a pilot's seat, the imperial
  colour of a corridor.
- **The character misreads other people.** Jordan's engine of dramatic irony: the point-of-view
  character interprets wrongly, and the reader sees it. She reads people by sensation — so she'll
  get it wrong. Often. That's a feature, not a bug.
- **The long-range plant.** Sow an offhand line, cash it in fifteen entries later. Keep the list of
  planted seeds (see Through-Line).
- **The long rhythm.** Stretches of calm — travel, repair, conversation — then a sudden, brief
  rupture. Never rush for the sake of pacing.

### What we don't take — and why it's an advantage

Both authors write in **third person, alternating points of view**. A first-person journal doesn't
have that tool. In exchange, it offers one that neither of them has:

**The reader is just as amnesiac as the character.**

No adversary point of view, no omniscience, no scene at the antagonist's table. The reader
discovers alongside her, gets it wrong alongside her, trusts the wrong people alongside her. The
discovery is permanent and shared. That's a tighter, rarer reading contract than Follett's
dramatic irony — and it's what will make a re-read feel violent.

Practical consequences:

1. **Write it hot**, in ignorance of what comes next. The entry closes on unease, not on a
   summary. Never "I would later understand that…".
2. **The File never leaks into the text.** It exists for you, not for the reader.
3. **The sixth line** is the one permitted exception: she says what she's holding back, without
   knowing why she's holding it back.
4. **The Encounter Sheet** creates the gap between feeling and truth. The reader will later compare
   *"what I believe is true"* against what turns out to have actually happened.

### Anatomy of a long entry

Five movements. Two or three suffice for a short entry.

1. **Anchor** — 2 concrete sentences. A place, a physical sensation, a time of day.
2. **Objective** — what she's come to do. Stated plainly.
3. **Friction** — a person, a resistance. This is where behaviour, appearance, the tic, the
   reaction all live.
4. **Cost** — what she loses, learns, or breaks. The change of state.
5. **Hook** — the double-meaning line. Just one. Never explained.

### Rhythm of a chapter

`calm — calm — tension — rupture — long calm — hook`

The rupture has to be **short**. Three paragraphs of violence beat ten. The reader remembers the
calm before it, not the wreckage.

### The rule of double meaning

**The mechanic never lies. He omits.** This is exactly the technique of Jordan's Aes Sedai: bound
by oath never to speak a false word, they become the best manipulators in the world. The
constraint *produces* the virtuosity. Every one of his lines has to be literally true and readable
a second way. *"I always knew you'd come back"* works as affection — and as the confession of
someone who knew what had been done to her hull.

Test before finalising a line: if the truth broke tomorrow, would this sentence still hold up? If
yes, it's good. If it becomes a lie, rewrite it.

### Writing scenes with Roskam

Lean on dialogue, not description. His scenes carry the story's humour and its central omission at
once, and both land better said aloud than summarised. Let him talk shop — hulls, hardpoints, FSD
grades, whatever's dusty in the hangar — at length; it's characterisation, not filler (see
"Procedural detail as proof of reality," above). Description is reserved for what dialogue can't
carry: the tic, a look that doesn't match the joke, the beat before a sentence he doesn't finish.
When he deflects a personal question, let the deflection be a real answer to a different question,
not a dodge the reader can catch him in — that's the double-meaning rule (above), applied to
evasion instead of affection.
